# Synthetic Data

`data/raw/process_instances.csv` and `data/raw/event_log.csv` are generated by
[`src/data/generate_data.py`](../src/data/generate_data.py):

```bash
python -m src.data.generate_data --n-processes 50000 --seed 42 \
    --start-date 2024-01-01 --end-date 2025-06-30
```

No proprietary data is used. The generator is a discrete-event-style
simulation of a loan application process, built so that every stage's
duration and every queueing delay is driven by an explicit, documented
business rule rather than pure randomness. This document explains those
rules so results can be interpreted correctly and reproduced.

## Process flow

```text
Application Submitted -> Document Validation -> Credit Assessment ->
Risk Review -> Approval -> Contract Generation -> Disbursement -> Completed
```

An application can be **rejected** (instead of completed) at Document
Validation, Credit Assessment, or Risk Review; the event log then ends at
that stage. Any stage can also produce a **rework** loop (e.g. `Document
Validation -> Document Rework -> Document Validation`) before moving on.

## Business hours and the calendar

Work only happens **Monday-Friday, 08:00-18:00** (`add_business_hours` in
`generate_data.py`). Both the service time and the queueing wait for a stage
are consumed as business-hour effort; if that effort would spill outside the
window it is carried over to the next business day. This is what produces:

- **Weekends have reduced/no processing capacity** — an application ready to
  be worked on Friday evening or over the weekend waits until Monday 08:00.
- A structural amplification from "hours of work" to "elapsed calendar time":
  only 10 of 24 hours/day and 5 of 7 days/week count as working time, so on
  average ~1 hour of effort costs **~3.4 elapsed hours**. This is why total
  cycle times (measured in days) look much longer than the sum of stage
  processing times (measured in hours) — it is a realistic property of any
  process with fixed business hours, not an artifact of the queueing model.

## Queueing / waiting time

Each stage has a **department** with a fixed number of employees
(`STAGE_CONFIG`), which combined with the stage's base service time gives a
daily capacity (`n_employees * 10 / base_hours`). For a given day, `utilization
= arrivals_that_day / capacity`. Waiting time is drawn from an M/M/1-style
curve:

```text
wait = base_wait + wait_scale * (utilization / (1 - utilization)) * noise
```

`arrivals_that_day` is a proxy: the count of *new applications submitted* on
that calendar date (known up front from the arrival schedule), used as a
stand-in for the department's workload around that date. This is a
documented simplification — a full multi-day backlog simulation was judged
unnecessary complexity for a portfolio project — but it is enough to produce
the required dynamics:

- **Certain days create queues**: arrivals are weighted so Monday and Friday
  are the busiest days (`WEEKDAY_ARRIVAL_WEIGHTS`), so utilization — and
  therefore waiting time — is highest right after the weekend backlog.
- **High workload increases waiting time**: built directly into the
  `utilization / (1 - utilization)` curve.
- **Some departments have higher utilization / Risk Review and Credit
  Assessment become bottlenecks under load**: Credit Assessment and Risk
  Review are staffed to run at higher average utilization (~0.45-0.50) than
  the other stages (~0.25-0.30), and their `wait_scale` is larger, so the
  same utilization produces more queueing there than elsewhere. Combined with
  their longer base service time, they consistently emerge as the top two
  bottlenecks in the generated data (see `data/raw/event_log.csv` — Credit
  Assessment's mean stage duration is roughly double Risk Review's, and both
  are far above the other stages).

## Service time

Each stage's service time is `base_hours * multiplier * lognormal_noise /
employee_speed`, where the multiplier combines:

- **Complexity** (`complexity_score`) — increases Document Validation, Credit
  Assessment and Contract Generation time.
- **Risk** (`risk_score`) — increases Credit Assessment and Risk Review time.
- **Large loan amount** (top decile for its application type) — adds a review
  surcharge to Risk Review and Approval ("large application amounts should
  require additional review").
- **Missing documents** — adds ~70% to Document Validation time and is the
  single biggest driver of Document Rework.
- **Application type** — mortgages and business loans carry an inherent
  handling multiplier (more underwriting/paperwork) on top of their
  complexity/risk scores.
- **Employee speed** — each employee has a personal speed multiplier
  (`N(1.0, 0.15)`, clipped to [0.65, 1.55]), so "some employees process
  applications faster than others" without any one employee being
  unrealistically fast or slow.

**High priority** applications get a tighter SLA target (`sla_target_hours *
0.85`) and are queue-jumped (wait time * 0.4) — expedited handling, not
magically faster processing.

## Rejection and rework

Whether an application is ultimately rejected, and at which stage, is decided
up front from a logistic function of risk score, missing documents, loan
size and complexity (`assign_rejections`), then simulated forward — so
rejected traces stop cleanly at their rejection stage rather than being
truncated after the fact. Rework probability is stage- and case-specific
(e.g. Document Validation rework jumps from an 8% base rate to ~43% when
documents are missing) and decays on repeated attempts, capped at 2 reworks
per stage to keep traces finite.

## Calibration

Target SLA breach rate: ~15-25% overall, with a realistic spread by risk
score, missing documents and application type (see the model validation
checks below). This required an iterative calibration loop — staffing levels
and SLA targets were tuned against the calendar-time amplification described
above until the simulated distribution matched that target; the current
`STAGE_CONFIG` and `SLA_BASE_HOURS` values in `generate_data.py` are the
result. Re-running with a different `--n-processes` or date range changes
average daily volume and therefore utilization, so these constants may need
re-tuning if the scale changes substantially.

## Known simplifications

- Department workload is approximated from same-day arrival volume rather
  than a true rolling backlog simulation (see above).
- Employees are assigned to a stage's queue uniformly at random, not by
  actual availability/schedule.
- Every process instance is independent; there is no shared, evolving queue
  state across instances (each instance's wait is drawn from the workload
  distribution for its arrival day, not from a live simulation clock).

These are reasonable simplifications for a portfolio-scale synthetic
dataset and are documented here so they are not mistaken for real
operational measurements.
